The Periodic Table is a Spec Sheet for Reality

Every element is a build. The nucleus sets the power budget, the electrons decide what it can connect to, and the table is the sorted inventory — ranked by atomic number, grouped by the shape of the outermost shell. Learn to read it properly and you can predict chemistry you have never seen before.

Orientation

How to read this table before you memorise anything

Most people meet the periodic table as a wall poster to be memorised. It is not. It is a lookup table generated by one rule — fill the lowest-energy orbital first — and almost everything else is a consequence.

Here is the compression: an atom's chemistry is governed almost entirely by its outermost electrons, because those are the only ones close enough to another atom to negotiate. The table is arranged so that atoms with the same outer-shell pattern sit in the same vertical column. That is the whole trick. Sodium and potassium behave alike not because someone decided they should, but because both end in a lone s electron sitting outside a closed, stable core.

Build analogy: character classes, not character stats

Think of a group (column) as a class — mage, tank, rogue. Everyone in the class has the same ability kit. Think of a period (row) as a tier — as you move down tiers, the same class gets bigger, heavier, and further from its core. A tier-6 mage and a tier-2 mage cast the same spell; one just has more mass and a longer reach.

Four coordinates you can read off any tile

Z
Atomic number — the count of protons. This is the element's identity and it is non-negotiable. Change Z and you have changed element.
Period
The row number equals the principal quantum number n of the outermost shell being filled. Period 3 means valence electrons live in n = 3.
Group
The column, which tracks the valence electron count and therefore the bonding behaviour.
Block
Which orbital type is being filled last: s, p, d, or f. This determines the shape of the chemistry more than anything else on the tile.

Module 1 · Epistemology and historical genesis

From pattern-matching to periodic law

The periodic table was not discovered in one flash. It was the output of about sixty years of people noticing that chemical properties repeat, and slowly working out what they repeat with respect to.

The intellectual problem in 1800 was brutal. Chemists had roughly thirty isolated substances that refused to decompose further, no theory of atomic structure, no concept of the electron, and no reliable way to measure atomic mass. They had reactions and they had weights. Everything else had to be inferred from pattern.

The pre-history: three failed-but-useful attempts

Döbereiner's triads (1829)

Johann Döbereiner noticed that certain groups of three elements had a suspicious arithmetic relationship: the middle element's atomic weight was approximately the mean of the other two. Calcium 40.1, strontium 87.6, barium 137.3 — and (40.1 + 137.3)/2 = 88.7, close to strontium's 87.6.

Why it mattered: it was the first quantitative claim that chemical similarity and atomic weight were connected. Why it failed: it only worked for a handful of triads and had no mechanism.

Newlands' law of octaves (1865)

John Newlands ordered the known elements by increasing atomic weight and found that every eighth element repeated properties, like notes on a musical scale. He was publicly mocked at the Chemical Society — one member asked whether he had tried arranging them alphabetically.

Why it mattered: the period-8 repeat is real for light elements (it is the 2 + 6 = 8 capacity of an s+p shell). Why it failed: it collapsed past calcium, because the d-block inserts ten extra elements Newlands had no room for.

Meyer's volume curve (1864–1870)

Lothar Meyer plotted atomic volume against atomic weight and got a striking sawtooth: sharp peaks at the alkali metals, deep troughs near the transition metals, repeating over and over. This was arguably the first published graph of periodicity, and it is essentially the same curve the trend plotter in Module 5 will draw for you.

Meyer and Mendeleev published within about a year of each other and both deserve credit. History remembers Mendeleev more because of one specific act of intellectual nerve, which is the subject of the next section.

The epistemological point

Every one of these people was ordering by atomic mass, which is the wrong variable. It correlates with the right variable well enough to reveal the pattern, but not well enough to make it exact. The whole history of the table between 1829 and 1913 is the story of a correct pattern found through a slightly wrong ordering parameter — and then rescued by Moseley.

Module 1.2

Mendeleev's predictive leap: betting on empty cells

Dmitri Mendeleev's 1869 table was not the first arrangement by weight. What made it science rather than bookkeeping was that he left holes in it and told everyone what would fill them.

Mendeleev's move was this: when an element did not fit the pattern, he assumed the pattern was right and the data was incomplete. Where the properties jumped awkwardly between two neighbours, he inserted a gap and declared that an undiscovered element belonged there. Then he did something far riskier — he predicted the missing element's atomic weight, density, oxide formula, chloride formula, and even how it would be isolated, by interpolating from its four neighbours.

He named these placeholders using Sanskrit numeral prefixes: eka (one), dvi (two). Eka-aluminium meant "one place below aluminium". Three of these predictions were confirmed within fifteen years, and the agreement was close enough to be uncomfortable.

Build analogy: inferring a stat block from the surrounding grid

Mendeleev did two-dimensional interpolation by hand. He took the element above, below, left and right of the hole, averaged their properties with some chemical judgement, and published the result as a falsifiable claim. It is the same logic as filling a missing cell in a spreadsheet from its row and column means — except the spreadsheet was the universe, and he was betting his reputation that the grid was real.

Eka-aluminium → gallium (1875), eka-boron → scandium (1879)

Gallium is the most famous case because of a dispute over density. Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran discovered gallium spectroscopically and measured its density at 4.7 g/cm³. Mendeleev, who had never seen the sample, wrote to say the measurement was wrong and that it should be about 5.9. Boisbaudran repurified the sample and remeasured: 5.904 g/cm³. A table had corrected a laboratory.

Where Mendeleev was wrong, and why it is instructive

  • He rejected the noble gases initially. When argon was isolated in 1894 with zero valence, there was no column for it. An entire new group had to be appended — which Mendeleev resisted before accepting it as Group 0.
  • He had pair inversions he could not justify. Tellurium (127.6) had to be placed before iodine (126.9) to keep the chemistry right, violating his own mass-ordering rule. He assumed the atomic weights were mismeasured. They were not — the resolution needed a variable he did not have.
  • He predicted elements that do not exist, including "coronium" and "newtonium" in a hypothetical group zero lighter than hydrogen.

The lesson worth keeping

A good model is not one that explains what you already have. It is one that makes specific, checkable claims about what you do not have yet — and survives them. Mendeleev's table is remembered because it was riskier than its competitors, not because it was tidier.

Module 1.3

Moseley's law: replacing mass with atomic number

In 1913 Henry Moseley fired electrons at metal targets, measured the X-rays that came back, and found the variable Mendeleev had been missing for forty years.

When a high-energy electron knocks out a 1s electron from a heavy atom, an electron from n = 2 drops into the hole and emits a characteristic X-ray photon — the Kα line. Moseley measured the frequency of that line for around forty elements and plotted the square root of the frequency against the element's position in the table. He got a straight line.

√ν = k₁ · (Z − σ) Moseley's law. ν is the Kα X-ray frequency, Z the atomic number, σ a screening constant ≈ 1 for the K series (the one remaining 1s electron screens the nucleus), and k₁ a constant ≈ 4.96 × 10⁷ Hz½ derived from the Rydberg constant.

Written out from the Bohr model, the Kα transition gives:

ν = (3/4) · R∞ · c · (Z − 1)² → √ν ∝ (Z − 1) The (3/4) factor is (1/1² − 1/2²). The square dependence on nuclear charge is why the relationship is so clean — X-ray energies are dominated by the nucleus, not by the messy outer electrons.

Why this settled everything

  • It gave an integer, physically meaningful ordering parameter. Z is a count of protons. There are no fractional protons, so there is no ambiguity about ordering.
  • It resolved the inversions. Tellurium has Z = 52 and iodine Z = 53, so tellurium genuinely comes first. The mass inversion happens because tellurium's natural isotope mix is unusually neutron-heavy. Mendeleev's chemical instinct was right and his ordering rule was wrong, and Moseley showed exactly why.
  • It counted the remaining gaps. Because Z is an integer, a missing line in the X-ray sequence means a missing element — full stop. Moseley showed that exactly four elements were still missing between aluminium and gold (Z = 43, 61, 72, 75: technetium, promethium, hafnium, rhenium). No more, no fewer.
  • It killed the rare-earth free-for-all. Claims of newly discovered rare earths could now be tested in an afternoon.

Periodic law, final form

The properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers, not their atomic weights. Every modern version of the table is sorted on Z.

Moseley was killed at Gallipoli in 1915, aged 27, two years after this work. It is widely regarded as one of the most costly single casualties in the history of science, and it is part of why several countries later adopted policies keeping prominent scientists out of combat roles.

Worked example: predicting an unknown Kα wavelength

Cobalt has Z = 27. Using ν = (3/4)·R∞·c·(Z − 1)² with R∞c = 3.29 × 10¹⁵ Hz:

ν = 0.75 × 3.29 × 10¹⁵ × (26)² = 0.75 × 3.29 × 10¹⁵ × 676 ≈ 1.668 × 10¹⁸ Hz

λ = c/ν = (3.00 × 10⁸)/(1.668 × 10¹⁸) ≈ 1.80 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 1.80 Å. The measured Co Kα is 1.79 Å. A two-line Bohr calculation lands within one percent — which tells you how dominant the nuclear charge term is at these energies.

Module 2 · Quantum mechanics and subatomic architecture

Anatomy of a tile, and the four numbers behind it

Before the physics, the interface. Every periodic table tile is a compressed data record, and once you know the fields you can read an element's behaviour without looking anything up.

Periods and groups are two different physical axes

Moving right along a period and moving down a group do completely different things to an atom. Horizontal movement adds a proton and an electron to the same shell; vertical movement opens an entirely new shell at a larger radius. Almost every trend in Module 5 is a consequence of this asymmetry.

The four quantum numbers: an addressing scheme

Solving the Schrödinger equation for a one-electron atom produces wavefunctions labelled by three integers, and experiment adds a fourth. Together they form a unique address for every electron in every atom. No two electrons in an atom can share all four — that is the Pauli exclusion principle, and it is the reason matter takes up space.

The table's shape is a picture of orbital capacity.
Module 2.2

Orbital geometry: what the electron cloud actually looks like

An orbital is not a track. It is a probability distribution — a map of where the electron is likely to be found, drawn at the boundary that encloses about 90 percent of the probability.

The single most useful sentence about orbital shape

Within the same shell, an s electron spends more time close to the nucleus than a p electron, which spends more time closer than a d electron. Closer means better shielded from nothing and more strongly attracted, so within a shell, energy rises s < p < d < f. Remember this and the filling order stops being arbitrary.

Module 2.3

The four blocks: the table is a map of orbital filling

Cut the periodic table into four regions by which orbital type is being filled last, and the strange staircase shape turns into something obvious.

Why 4s fills before 3d

Energy ordering is not set by n alone. The 4s orbital penetrates the inner shells more deeply than 3d does, so it experiences a higher effective nuclear charge and sits slightly lower in energy in a neutral, unfilled atom. Once electrons are actually in the 3d orbitals, the ordering flips: in cations, 3d is below 4s.

This is why transition metals lose 4s electrons first when ionising. Fe → Fe²⁺ is [Ar]3d⁶4s² → [Ar]3d⁶, not [Ar]3d⁴4s².

The Madelung / n + ℓ rule

Orbitals fill in order of increasing n + ℓ. When two orbitals tie on n + ℓ, the one with lower n fills first.

4s has n + ℓ = 4 + 0 = 4. 3d has n + ℓ = 3 + 2 = 5. So 4s comes first. Similarly 6s (6) beats 4f (7) beats 5d (7, but higher n... and 5d has lower n+ℓ tie-break against 6p). The rule reproduces the observed order for nearly every neutral atom.

Module 2.4

Configuration rules: Aufbau, Pauli, Hund

Three rules generate the electron configuration of any element in the ground state. They are not independent — each answers a different question.

Aufbau principle

Which orbital next? Electrons occupy the lowest-energy available orbital first. Build up from the bottom of the energy ladder.

Pauli exclusion principle

How many fit? No two electrons in an atom may share all four quantum numbers, so each orbital caps at two electrons with opposite spins.

Hund's rule of maximum multiplicity

How do they arrange? Within a degenerate set, electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before any pairing occurs.

Hund's rule, properly explained

Textbooks usually say electrons "want to spread out because they repel", which is only half the reason and not the interesting half. There are two real terms:

  1. Coulomb repulsion. Two electrons in the same orbital occupy the same region of space and repel each other strongly. Putting them in different orbitals of the same subshell costs nothing in orbital energy and saves this pairing energy.
  2. Exchange energy. This is the quantum-mechanical part and it dominates. Electrons with parallel spins in different orbitals are forbidden by Pauli from ever being in the same place, so they avoid each other automatically. Every pair of parallel-spin electrons contributes a stabilising exchange term K. The number of such pairs for n parallel electrons is n(n−1)/2, so the stabilisation grows quadratically.
Eexchange = −K · N(N−1)/2 N = number of parallel-spin electrons in a degenerate set N=3 → 3 pairs N=5 → 10 pairs N=7 → 21 pairs This quadratic growth is why half-filled subshells (d⁵, f⁷) are exceptionally stable: they maximise the number of parallel pairs before any spin has to flip.

Build analogy: seats on a bus

Passengers boarding a bus take an empty double seat before sitting next to a stranger. Hund's rule is that, plus a discount: passengers facing the same direction as each other get a group rate that scales with how many of them there are. Half-filled means every seat has exactly one person and everyone is claiming the maximum group discount.

Worked example: write the full configuration of bromine (Z = 35)

Follow the Aufbau diagonal, filling to capacity, keeping a running total:

1s² (2) 2s² (4) 2p⁶ (10) 3s² (12) 3p⁶ (18) 4s² (20) 3d¹⁰ (30) 4p⁵ (35)

Full: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶4s²3d¹⁰4p⁵. Noble-gas shorthand: [Ar]3d¹⁰4s²4p⁵ (note the convention of writing 4s before 3d in the final answer, ordered by n, even though 4s filled first).

Reading the chemistry straight off: 7 valence electrons (4s² 4p⁵), one short of a closed octet. Period 4, group 17. Expect an aggressive one-electron oxidant forming Br⁻ and diatomic Br₂. All correct.

Worked example: configuration of an ion — Fe³⁺

Neutral Fe: [Ar]3d⁶4s². Remove three electrons. Rule: highest n first, so the 4s pair goes before any 3d.

Fe³⁺ = [Ar]3d⁵ — a half-filled d subshell, which is why Fe³⁺ is so much more stable than you would naively expect and why iron(III) chemistry is everywhere.

Module 2.5

Exceptional configurations: chromium, copper, and the half-filled discount

About twenty elements break the naive Aufbau prediction. The two you must be able to explain are chromium and copper, because they show the two competing energy terms in their cleanest form.

Chromium (Z = 24)

Naive prediction: [Ar]3d⁴4s²
Observed: [Ar]3d⁵4s¹

Promoting one 4s electron into 3d costs a small orbital-energy penalty ΔE, because 3d sits slightly above 4s in the neutral atom. It buys three things: a half-filled d⁵ set with five parallel spins (10 exchange pairs instead of 6), removal of one 4s–4s pairing repulsion, and lower electron–electron repulsion overall because the two remaining lone electrons are in more spatially separated orbitals.

exchange pairs: d⁴s² → 6 + 0 = 6 d⁵s¹ → 10 + 0 = 10 net gain ≈ 4K − ΔE(4s→3d) − small > 0

Copper (Z = 29)

Naive prediction: [Ar]3d⁹4s²
Observed: [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹

Here the prize is a completely filled d subshell. A full d¹⁰ shell is spherically symmetric, which minimises electron–electron repulsion and maximises the total exchange stabilisation available (two full sets of five parallel spins). The lone 4s¹ electron left outside is poorly shielded by the compact d¹⁰ core — which is exactly why copper is such a good conductor and why Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺ are both accessible.

d⁹s² → 10 + 6 = 16 pairs d¹⁰s¹ → 10 + 10 = 20 pairs

What your exam actually wants

Do not say "d⁵ and d¹⁰ are more stable" and stop. That is the observation, not the explanation. Say: the promotion is favourable because the exchange stabilisation gained from maximising parallel spins, plus the reduction in pairing repulsion, exceeds the small orbital-energy cost of moving an electron from 4s to 3d, since those orbitals are nearly degenerate in the neutral atom. That sentence is worth full marks.

Honest caveat, because rigour matters

The "half-filled shells are stable" story is a useful heuristic, not a complete theory. Modern computational work shows the true driver in Cr and Cu is a subtle balance of exchange, correlation, and 3d–4s radial overlap, and the heuristic fails outright for several 4d and 5d elements where relativistic effects and near-degeneracy dominate. Palladium ending in 4d¹⁰5s⁰ cannot be explained by the half-filled rule at all.

Module 3 · Nuclear forces and shielding physics

Effective nuclear charge: the number that runs the whole table

A valence electron in sodium does not feel eleven protons. It feels about two and a half. Understanding why is the difference between memorising periodic trends and deriving them.

The nucleus pulls every electron inward with a force proportional to Z. But the other electrons push back, and more importantly, the ones sitting between a given electron and the nucleus cancel part of the nuclear charge before it is ever felt. The residual attraction is the effective nuclear charge:

Zeff = Z − S Z = actual proton count. S = shielding (screening) constant, the fraction of nuclear charge neutralised by other electrons. Zeff is what the electron in question actually experiences.

Two electrons shield very differently depending on where they are. A core electron, deep inside and always between the valence electron and the nucleus, shields almost perfectly — about 1.00 charge units each. A same-shell electron is beside the valence electron rather than under it, so it shields poorly — roughly 0.35 units. This asymmetry is the engine of periodicity.

Build analogy: signal attenuation through walls

The nucleus is a Wi-Fi router broadcasting at full power. Core electrons are concrete walls; same-shell electrons are people standing next to you. Walls kill the signal almost completely; bystanders barely attenuate it. Zeff is your actual bar count, and every periodic trend is a question about how many walls are between you and the router.

The two directions, derived rather than memorised

Across a period → Zeff rises sharply

Each step right adds one proton (+1.00 to Z) but only one same-shell electron (+0.35 to S). Net change in Zeff ≈ +0.65 per step. Across period 3, Zeff on a valence electron climbs from about 2.20 (Na) to about 6.10 (Cl). The valence shell is pulled progressively tighter — so atoms shrink, ionisation energy rises, and electronegativity rises.

Down a group → Zeff is nearly flat

Each step down adds a full shell of new core electrons that shield almost perfectly, so the added protons are almost entirely cancelled. Zeff on the valence electron creeps up only slowly (Li 1.30, Na 2.20, K 2.20, Rb 2.20 under Slater's simple rules). Meanwhile n increases, so the valence electron is much further out. Distance wins: atoms get bigger and ionisation energy falls.

Module 3.2

Slater's rules: the algorithm, step by step

John C. Slater published an empirical recipe in 1930 that estimates S well enough to reproduce real trends. It is arithmetic, it takes ninety seconds, and it is examinable everywhere.

Step 1 — Write the configuration in Slater groupings

Rewrite the electron configuration in this specific order and bracketing, grouping s with p at the same n but keeping d and f separate:

(1s) (2s,2p) (3s,3p) (3d) (4s,4p) (4d) (4f) (5s,5p) (5d) … Critically: s and p of the same shell share a group; d and f each get their own. The ordering is by n, then by ℓ.

The mistake almost everyone makes

For a d or f electron, every electron in an inner group counts a full 1.00 — not 0.85. There is no n−1 discount for d electrons. This is because d orbitals penetrate poorly, so inner electrons block them almost completely. Getting this wrong flips the Fe 3d vs 4s comparison, which is the classic exam question.

Step 3 — Worked examples

Example A · Silicon (Z = 14), a 3p valence electron

Configuration in Slater groups: (1s²)(2s²2p⁶)(3s²3p²)

  • Same group (3s,3p): 4 electrons total, minus the one in question = 3 × 0.35 = 1.05
  • n − 1 shell (2s,2p): 8 × 0.85 = 6.80
  • n − 2 and deeper (1s): 2 × 1.00 = 2.00

S = 1.05 + 6.80 + 2.00 = 9.85 → Zeff = 14 − 9.85 = 4.15

Example B · Sodium (Z = 11) vs. chlorine (Z = 17) — deriving the size difference

Na, 3s electron. (1s²)(2s²2p⁶)(3s¹) → S = (0 × 0.35) + (8 × 0.85) + (2 × 1.00) = 8.80. Zeff = 2.20

Cl, 3p electron. (1s²)(2s²2p⁶)(3s²3p⁵) → S = (6 × 0.35) + (8 × 0.85) + (2 × 1.00) = 2.10 + 6.80 + 2.00 = 10.90. Zeff = 6.10

Chlorine's valence electrons are held by nearly three times the effective charge of sodium's, in the same principal shell. Measured atomic radii: Na 186 pm, Cl 99 pm. The arithmetic predicted the shrinkage before you looked it up.

Example C · Iron (Z = 26) — why the 4s electron leaves first

Configuration in Slater groups: (1s²)(2s²2p⁶)(3s²3p⁶)(3d⁶)(4s²)

For a 4s electron: same group (4s): 1 × 0.35 = 0.35; shell n−1 (3s,3p,3d = 14 electrons): 14 × 0.85 = 11.90; n−2 and deeper (10 electrons): 10 × 1.00 = 10.00. S = 22.25 → Zeff = 26 − 22.25 = 3.75

For a 3d electron: same group (3d, 5 others): 5 × 0.35 = 1.75; everything to the left (18 electrons): 18 × 1.00 = 18.00. S = 19.75 → Zeff = 26 − 19.75 = 6.25

The 3d electron is held by Zeff = 6.25; the 4s electron by only 3.75. The 4s electron is the loosely-held one, so it ionises first. This is the quantitative answer to "why does Fe²⁺ have configuration [Ar]3d⁶ rather than [Ar]3d⁴4s²".

Example D · Comparing 1s electrons: helium vs. hydrogen

H (Z=1): one electron, nothing to shield it. S = 0, Zeff = 1.00.

He (Z=2): the other 1s electron shields by the special value 0.30. S = 0.30, Zeff = 1.70.

Helium's electrons feel 70 percent more nuclear pull than hydrogen's — which is why helium has the highest first ionisation energy of any element (2,372 kJ/mol) and the smallest atomic radius (31 pm).

Where Slater's rules break down

They are a 1930 empirical fit, not a theory. They ignore the distinction between s and p within a shell, they take no account of relativistic effects (so they are unreliable past about Z = 70), and they give identical Zeff for Na, K and Rb valence electrons when the true values drift upward. For accurate work, chemists use Clementi–Raimondi values from Hartree–Fock calculations. Use Slater for reasoning and trend prediction; use tabulated values for numbers that matter.

Module 4.1 · Group 1

Alkali metals: one electron, no hesitation

ns¹oxidation +1Li Na K Rb Cs Frlowest IE₁ in each period

Every alkali metal has exactly one electron outside a noble gas core. That electron is shielded by a nearly complete set of core electrons, so its Zeff is around 2.2, and losing it produces a closed-shell cation. There is no version of alkali chemistry that is not "give away the s electron immediately".

Reactivity, and why it accelerates downward

Going down the group the valence electron sits in a higher shell, further from a nucleus whose extra charge is fully screened. Ionisation energy falls monotonically — Li 520, Na 496, K 419, Rb 403, Cs 376 kJ/mol — and reactivity rises accordingly. The classic demonstration is reaction with water:

2 M(s) + 2 H₂O(l) → 2 MOH(aq) + H₂(g) M = Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs Li: steady fizzing Na: melts into a ball, may ignite K: lilac flame, ignites the H₂ Rb: violent Cs: explosive, shatters glass on contact

Lithium's kinetic anomaly

Lithium has the most negative standard reduction potential of the group (−3.04 V), meaning thermodynamically it is the strongest reducing agent — yet it reacts with water the most gently. The resolution: E° includes the large hydration enthalpy of the small Li⁺ ion, which is a solution-phase property, while the visible violence of the reaction is governed by kinetics and by the metal's high melting point (180 °C, so it never liquefies and exposes fresh surface). Thermodynamic favourability and observed rate are different questions.

Solvation in liquid ammonia: the blue electron soup

Dissolve sodium in liquid ammonia at −33 °C and you do not get a normal metal solution. You get a deep blue, paramagnetic, electrically conducting liquid containing solvated electrons — free electrons stabilised in cavities of ammonia molecules.

Na(s) + (x+y) NH₃(l) → [Na(NH₃)ₓ]⁺ + [e(NH₃)_y]⁻ At high concentrations the solution turns bronze and conducts nearly as well as liquid mercury. These solutions are the standard reagent for Birch reduction, which selectively reduces aromatic rings — a reaction no other reagent performs as cleanly.

Engineering: lithium-ion battery chemistry

Lithium is chosen for batteries for three reasons that are all periodic-table consequences: it has the lowest standard reduction potential of any element (maximum cell voltage), the lowest density of any metal (0.53 g/cm³, so maximum energy per kilogram), and the smallest cation radius (76 pm, so it can physically slot into host lattices).

Cathode: LiCoO₂ ⇌ Li₁₋ₓCoO₂ + x Li⁺ + x e⁻ Anode: C₆ + x Li⁺ + x e⁻ ⇌ LiₓC₆ Cell: LiCoO₂ + C₆ ⇌ Li₁₋ₓCoO₂ + LiₓC₆ (≈ 3.7 V nominal) Nothing is consumed — Li⁺ shuttles between two host structures, which is why the cell is rechargeable. This is called intercalation chemistry, and cobalt's variable oxidation state (Co³⁺/Co⁴⁺) is what balances the charge.

Flame colours and the emission mechanism

Heat an alkali salt and the valence electron is promoted to a higher orbital; when it falls back, the energy difference is emitted as a photon of a specific wavelength. Because each element has a unique orbital energy spacing, each gives a unique colour — this is the physical basis of atomic emission spectroscopy and of every fireworks display.

Safety

Alkali metals are stored under mineral oil because they react with atmospheric moisture and oxygen. Caesium and rubidium ignite spontaneously in air. Never quench an alkali metal fire with water or CO₂ — both react. Use dry sand or a Class D extinguisher. Lithium battery fires are a separate hazard class: thermal runaway is self-sustaining, produces its own oxygen from cathode decomposition, and can reignite hours later.

Module 4.2 · Group 2

Alkaline earth metals: two electrons, and the lattice energy payoff

ns²oxidation +2Be Mg Ca Sr Ba Raharder, denser, higher mp than Group 1

Removing two electrons costs much more than removing one — magnesium's IE₁ is 738 kJ/mol and its IE₂ is 1,451 kJ/mol, so forming Mg²⁺ costs 2,189 kJ/mol against just 738 for a hypothetical Mg⁺. Yet Mg²⁺ is what forms, universally. The reason is not ionisation energy at all; it is what happens afterwards.

Why +2 always wins: lattice energy

Ulattice ∝ (q₊ × q₋) / (r₊ + r₋) Born–Landé proportionality. Doubling the cation charge roughly doubles the numerator, and a 2+ cation is also smaller than a 1+ cation of the same element, shrinking the denominator. MgO has a lattice energy near −3,795 kJ/mol; NaCl is −787. That extra binding energy pays back the second ionisation many times over.

This single relationship explains the entire personality of Group 2: high melting points (MgO melts at 2,852 °C and is used as furnace refractory), low solubility for many salts, and hard, structural metals rather than soft ones.

Biochemical signalling: Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ as the body's messaging bus

Calcium is not just structural. Cytosolic Ca²⁺ is held about ten thousand times lower than extracellular Ca²⁺ (roughly 100 nM inside versus 1–2 mM outside) by constantly-running ATP-driven pumps. That enormous maintained gradient means opening a calcium channel for milliseconds produces an instant, unmistakable concentration spike — a perfect digital signal.

  • Muscle contraction: Ca²⁺ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum binds troponin C, moving tropomyosin off the actin binding sites and allowing the myosin power stroke.
  • Neurotransmission: depolarisation opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels at the axon terminal; the influx triggers vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release.
  • Magnesium's role: Mg²⁺ is the counter-ion for ATP. The biologically active species is Mg·ATP²⁻, not free ATP⁴⁻ — magnesium neutralises the phosphate charge so that enzymes can position it. Roughly three hundred enzymes require it.
  • Chlorophyll: a porphyrin ring with Mg²⁺ at the centre. Swap in Fe²⁺ and you have haem instead — the same architectural motif, a different metal, a completely different job.

Build analogy: interrupt signalling

The cell keeps internal calcium near zero the way a system keeps an interrupt line held low. Firing the signal costs almost nothing because the gradient is already loaded — you just open the gate. The expensive part is the pumping that maintains the gradient in the background, which is exactly how much of cellular energy is actually spent.

Safety and the beryllium exception

Beryllium is the outlier of the group: small, high charge density, and covalent rather than ionic in most of its bonding (BeCl₂ is a covalent polymer, not a salt). It is also acutely toxic — inhaled beryllium dust causes chronic beryllium disease, an irreversible granulomatous lung condition. Barium compounds are toxic if soluble; BaSO₄ is safe to ingest only because it is not.

Module 4.3 · The d-block

Transition metals: variable oxidation states and colour

The strict IUPAC definition of a transition metal is an element that forms at least one ion with a partially filled d subshell. This deliberately excludes zinc, cadmium and mercury, whose only common ions are d¹⁰ — which is why zinc compounds are white and boring while copper compounds are blue and interesting.

Why oxidation states are variable

In main-group elements the energy gap between valence and core is enormous, so there is exactly one sensible thing to lose. In the d-block, the 4s and 3d orbitals are close in energy, so several electrons are available at similar cost. Manganese demonstrates the full span:

A colour subtlety worth knowing

Permanganate is d⁰ — it has no d electrons — so its intense purple cannot come from d–d transitions. It comes from ligand-to-metal charge transfer: a photon promotes an electron from an oxygen lone pair onto the metal. LMCT transitions are Laporte-allowed, which is why permanganate is roughly a thousand times more intensely coloured than a typical d–d complex like [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺. If a compound is shockingly vivid, suspect charge transfer, not crystal field splitting.

Crystal field theory: where the colour comes from

An isolated metal ion has five degenerate d orbitals. Surround it with six ligands along the x, y, z axes (an octahedral complex) and the degeneracy breaks. The dz² and dx²−y² orbitals point directly at the incoming ligands and are destabilised; the dxy, dxz, dyz orbitals point between them and are stabilised.

Octahedral splitting: t₂g (3 orbitals, −0.4 Δo) e_g (2 orbitals, +0.6 Δo) Colour absorbed: λ = hc / Δo Colour seen = complementary of colour absorbed CFSE = (−0.4 × n_t2g + 0.6 × n_eg) Δo + (pairing corrections) Δo for [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ corresponds to absorption around 800 nm (red/near-IR), so the transmitted light is blue — the colour of copper sulfate solution.

The size of Δo depends on the ligand, giving the spectrochemical series, ordered from weak to strong field:

I⁻ < Br⁻ < S²⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < H₂O < NH₃ < en < NO₂⁻ < CN⁻ < CO Swap water for ammonia in a copper complex and the solution goes from pale blue to deep royal blue — Δo increased, the absorbed wavelength shortened, the perceived colour shifted. Strong-field ligands can also force low-spin configurations when Δo exceeds the pairing energy P.

Build analogy: colour as a bandpass filter

Δo sets the exact photon energy the complex can absorb — everything else passes through. Changing the ligand is retuning the filter's centre frequency. You are not adding pigment; you are subtracting a narrow band from white light, and your eye reports what survived.

Industrial catalysis: the Haber–Bosch process

The single most consequential chemical process ever industrialised. It fixes atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia, feeds an estimated half the world's population through synthetic fertiliser, and consumes roughly 1–2 percent of global energy output.

N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) ⇌ 2 NH₃(g) ΔH = −92 kJ/mol Conditions: 400–450 °C, 150–250 atm, Fe catalyst promoted with K₂O and Al₂O₃ Le Chatelier says low temperature and high pressure maximise yield, but at low temperature the rate is unusably slow. The 450 °C setting is a deliberate compromise: a lower equilibrium yield achieved fast enough to matter, with unreacted gas recycled. This is the canonical example of kinetics overriding thermodynamics in process design.

The iron catalyst works because transition metals can form bonds of intermediate strength. The N≡N triple bond (945 kJ/mol) is one of the strongest in chemistry; the iron surface chemisorbs N₂ and weakens it enough for H atoms to attack sequentially. A catalyst that bound nitrogen too weakly would not activate it; one that bound too strongly would never release the product. This optimum is the Sabatier principle, and plotting activity against binding strength gives the famous volcano curve with iron and ruthenium near the peak.

Hexavalent chromium (Cr⁶⁺) is a confirmed human carcinogen and the contaminant at issue in the Hinkley groundwater case. Nickel is the most common contact allergen in the world. Module 4.4 · p-block metals and the staircase

Post-transition metals and metalloids: the semiconductor frontier

B Si Ge As Sb Teamphoteric oxidesinert pair effectdoping

The staircase running from boron down to astatine separates metals from nonmetals, and the elements sitting on it are neither. Metalloids have partly filled bands and intermediate electronegativity, which makes them conduct — but only conditionally. That conditionality is the entire semiconductor industry.

The inert pair effect

Heavy p-block elements increasingly prefer an oxidation state two lower than their group would suggest. Thallium prefers +1 over +3; lead prefers +2 over +4; bismuth prefers +3 over +5. The ns² pair becomes reluctant to participate in bonding.

Two reinforcing causes. First, relativistic contraction (Module 6) stabilises and shrinks the 6s orbital, lowering its energy and increasing its ionisation energy. Second, bond enthalpies decrease down the group, so the energy released by forming two extra bonds no longer repays the ionisation cost. The classic consequence: PbO₂ is a powerful oxidant because Pb⁴⁺ desperately wants to become Pb²⁺ — which is precisely the reaction a lead-acid car battery exploits.

Lead-acid discharge: Anode (−): Pb + HSO₄⁻ → PbSO₄ + H⁺ + 2e⁻ E° = +0.36 V Cathode (+): PbO₂ + 3H⁺ + HSO₄⁻ + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2H₂O E° = +1.69 V Cell: 2.05 V per cell × 6 cells = 12.3 V Lead is one of the few elements where both oxidation states form an insoluble sulfate that stays put on the electrode — which is why the reaction is reversible and the battery rechargeable.

Amphoteric behaviour

Elements near the metal/nonmetal boundary form oxides that react with both acids and bases. Aluminium is the textbook case:

Al₂O₃ + 6 HCl → 2 AlCl₃ + 3 H₂O (behaving as a base) Al₂O₃ + 2 NaOH + 3 H₂O → 2 Na[Al(OH)₄] (behaving as an acid) The second reaction is the basis of the Bayer process for refining bauxite. Zinc oxide, lead oxide, tin oxide and beryllium oxide behave the same way.

Doping physics: how you turn sand into a transistor

Pure silicon is a poor conductor — a full valence band, an empty conduction band, and a 1.12 eV gap between them. Doping introduces deliberate impurities that place carriers inside the gap.

n-type: add a Group 15 atom

Phosphorus has five valence electrons. Four bond into the silicon lattice; the fifth is left over, sitting in a donor level just ~45 meV below the conduction band. At room temperature (kT ≈ 25 meV) it is easily promoted. Carriers are negative electrons.

p-type: add a Group 13 atom

Boron has three valence electrons — one short. The missing bond is a hole, an acceptor level just above the valence band. Electrons hop into it, moving the hole the other way. Carriers behave as mobile positive charges.

Join p-type and n-type silicon and you get a junction that conducts in one direction only: a diode. Sandwich three layers and modulate the middle one and you get a transistor. Every logic gate in every processor is built from this one periodic-table trick — Group 13 and Group 15 dopants on either side of Group 14.

Reactive nonmetals and halogens: the electron collectors

Halogens are one electron short of a closed shell and sit at the highest Zeff values in their periods. Fluorine is the most electronegative element at 3.98 on the Pauling scale, and no element has ever been shown to oxidise it.

Disinfection mechanics

Chlorine in water establishes an equilibrium producing hypochlorous acid, which is the actual biocide:

Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HOCl + H⁺ + Cl⁻ HOCl ⇌ H⁺ + OCl⁻ (pKa = 7.53) HOCl is 80–100× more effective than OCl⁻ because it is neutral and can diffuse through the bacterial cell membrane, where it oxidises sulfhydryl groups in essential enzymes. Since the pKa is 7.53, pool pH control is not cosmetic — at pH 8 most of your chlorine is sitting in the far weaker OCl⁻ form.

The halogen cycle in lamps

A plain tungsten filament evaporates and deposits on the cooler glass, blackening the bulb and thinning the filament until it fails. Add a trace of iodine or bromine and you get a regenerative cycle:

Near the wall (~500 °C): W(g) + 2 I₂(g) → WI₄(g) Near the filament (>1400 °C): WI₄(g) → W(s) + 2 I₂(g) Tungsten is transported off the glass and redeposited on the filament. This lets the filament run hotter — around 3,000 K instead of 2,700 K — which shifts the blackbody peak toward visible wavelengths, raising efficacy and colour temperature. The tradeoff is that the envelope must be quartz to survive the heat, which is why halogen bulbs must never be handled with bare fingers: skin oils cause devitrification and failure.

The other reactive nonmetals

  • Oxygen (Group 16): the second most electronegative element. Paramagnetic as O₂ — a fact that Lewis structures cannot explain and molecular orbital theory predicts immediately from two unpaired electrons in π* orbitals.
  • Nitrogen (Group 15): the N≡N bond at 945 kJ/mol makes atmospheric nitrogen almost inert, which is why nitrogen fixation is so energetically expensive and why nitrogen-rich explosives release so much energy when they form it.
  • Carbon (Group 14): uniquely capable of catenation — long chains of C–C bonds at 346 kJ/mol each, comparable to C–O. Silicon cannot compete because Si–Si (222) is far weaker than Si–O (452), which is why Earth's silicon is locked in rock while its carbon runs biology.

Safety

Fluorine gas ignites almost everything, including asbestos and water. Hydrofluoric acid is a weak acid that is nonetheless among the most dangerous laboratory chemicals: the small neutral HF molecule penetrates skin painlessly, then F⁻ sequesters calcium and magnesium in tissue, causing deep necrosis and potentially fatal hypocalcaemia from exposures as small as 2 percent of body surface area. Calcium gluconate gel is the specific antidote and must be on hand before HF is opened.

Module 4.6 · Group 18

Noble gases: closed shells, and the exceptions that prove the rule

ns²np⁶He Ne Ar Kr Xe Rn Oghighest IE in each period

A full ns²np⁶ octet gives a spherically symmetric electron cloud, maximum Zeff for the period, and no energetically accessible way to gain or lose an electron. Helium's IE₁ of 2,372 kJ/mol is the highest of any element. For seventy years these were called the inert gases, and the belief that they formed no compounds was absolute.

Breaking xenon

In 1962 Neil Bartlett noticed that PtF₆ oxidised molecular oxygen to O₂⁺[PtF₆]⁻. He checked the numbers: O₂ has an ionisation energy of 1,175 kJ/mol; xenon's is 1,170 — slightly lower. If PtF₆ could strip an electron from oxygen, it should be able to strip one from xenon. He mixed the gases and got an immediate orange-yellow solid.

Xe + PtF₆ → Xe⁺[PtF₆]⁻ (Bartlett, 1962) Xe + F₂ → XeF₂ (400 °C, Ni vessel) Xe + 2F₂ → XeF₄ (400 °C, 6 atm, 1:5 Xe:F₂) Xe + 3F₂ → XeF₆ (300 °C, 60 atm, excess F₂) XeF₆ + 3H₂O → XeO₃ + 6HF (XeO₃ is a shock-sensitive high explosive) Xenon works because it is large: its valence electrons are far from the nucleus and well shielded, so its ionisation energy has dropped enough for an extremely aggressive oxidant to reach. Helium and neon remain genuinely compound-free.

The geometry of these compounds is a favourite exam target because the lone pairs matter. XeF₄ has six electron domains (four bonding, two lone pairs), giving octahedral electron geometry; the lone pairs take opposite axial positions, leaving a square planar molecule. XeF₂ has five domains with three equatorial lone pairs, giving a linear molecule.

Module 4.7 · The f-block

Lanthanides and actinides: buried orbitals, enormous consequences

4f / 5fCe–Lu, Th–LrLn³⁺ dominantall actinides radioactive

The 4f orbitals fill after 6s and are radially inside the 5s and 5p shells. They are shielded from the chemical environment by the electrons above them, which produces the defining fact about lanthanides: the number of 4f electrons barely affects their chemistry. Every lanthanide behaves like a slightly-different-sized trivalent cation. This is why they occur together in the same ores and why separating them was one of the hardest problems in nineteenth-century chemistry.

The lanthanide contraction

Across the fifteen lanthanides, each added 4f electron shields the added proton poorly — diffuse, non-penetrating f orbitals are bad screens. Zeff therefore creeps upward the whole way across, and the ionic radius shrinks steadily from La³⁺ (103 pm) to Lu³⁺ (86 pm), a 17 percent reduction.

Magnetism: why neodymium magnets exist

Lanthanide ions can carry very large numbers of unpaired f electrons — up to seven in Gd³⁺ (4f⁷). More importantly, because 4f orbitals are shielded, their orbital angular momentum is not quenched by the crystal field the way it is in 3d transition metals. That surviving orbital contribution produces enormous magnetic anisotropy: the magnetic moment strongly prefers one crystallographic axis.

Nd₂Fe₁₄B · energy product (BH)max ≈ 400–440 kJ/m³ · ~10× ferrite Design split: Nd supplies anisotropy (coercivity), Fe supplies magnetisation (strength), B stabilises the tetragonal crystal structure. Weakness: Curie temperature only ~583 K, so Dy is added to survive EV motor heat. Coercivity is resistance to being demagnetised; without the lanthanide's anisotropy a strong magnet would simply flip its domains under load. This is why you cannot build a compact high-torque motor, a hard drive actuator, or a small high-fidelity speaker without rare earths.

Actinides and nuclear fission

The 5f orbitals are less buried than 4f, so early actinides show variable oxidation states like transition metals (uranium reaches +6 as the uranyl ion UO₂²⁺). Every actinide is radioactive; only thorium and uranium exist in usable primordial quantities.

²³⁵U + n(thermal) → fission fragments + 2.4 n(avg) + ~200 MeV Natural uranium: 99.27% ²³⁸U, 0.72% ²³⁵U, trace ²³⁴U Reactor fuel: 3–5% ²³⁵U Weapons-grade: >90% Breeding: ²³⁸U + n → ²³⁹U →(β⁻, 23 min)→ ²³⁹Np →(β⁻, 2.4 d)→ ²³⁹Pu ²³⁵U is fissile because it has an odd neutron count — absorbing a neutron gives an even-even nucleus and releases pairing energy, pushing it over the fission barrier without any kinetic energy input. ²³⁸U needs a fast neutron (>1 MeV) to fission, which is why it is fertile rather than fissile.

Decay series

Heavy nuclei decay in chains until they reach a stable isotope. The uranium series is 238U → (14 steps, 4.47 billion year half-life on the first) → 206Pb. Every step is an alpha or beta emission, and the chain passes through radium and radon — which is why radon accumulates in basements built on granite. Because alpha decay reduces mass number by 4 and beta decay leaves it unchanged, every nucleus in a given chain has mass number congruent modulo 4, giving exactly four possible decay families (4n, 4n+1, 4n+2, 4n+3).

Safety and supply

Rare earths are not geologically rare — cerium is more abundant in the crust than copper. They are chemically difficult, requiring hundreds of sequential solvent-extraction stages to separate ions that differ by a few picometres, and the ores often carry thorium, creating radioactive tailings. The processing bottleneck, not the geology, is why rare earth supply is strategically concentrated.

Module 5.2

Atomic and ionic radius

An atom has no hard edge, so "radius" is always operationally defined. Three definitions are in common use and they do not agree: covalent radius (half the distance between two bonded identical atoms), metallic radius (half the nearest-neighbour distance in the solid metal), and van der Waals radius (half the closest approach of two non-bonded atoms). Always check which one a table is reporting before comparing numbers across sources.

Cations shrink, anions swell

Cation formation

Na (186 pm) → Na⁺ (102 pm), a 45 percent reduction. Two effects: the entire outer shell is often removed (Na loses all of n = 3), and the remaining electrons are shared among the same number of protons, so each is pulled in harder.

Anion formation

Cl (99 pm) → Cl⁻ (181 pm), an 83 percent increase. No new shell is added — 17 protons must now hold 18 electrons, so Zeff per electron drops and mutual repulsion expands the cloud.

Isoelectronic series: same electrons, different nuclei

An isoelectronic series is a set of ions with identical electron configurations. Because the electron count and shielding are constant, size depends on nuclear charge alone — making this the cleanest possible demonstration that Zeff controls radius.

Practice: rank N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, Mg²⁺ by size

All have 10 electrons. Rank by Z ascending → radius descending: N³⁻ (Z=7, 146 pm) > O²⁻ (8, 140) > F⁻ (9, 133) > Ne (10, 69 van der Waals, not directly comparable) > Na⁺ (11, 102) > Mg²⁺ (12, 72). Note the trap: neutral neon is usually excluded from these rankings because its radius is measured by a different convention.

Module 5.3

Ionisation energy: reading the ladder

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻ ΔH = IE₁ (always endothermic, always gas phase) X⁺(g) → X²⁺(g) + e⁻ ΔH = IE₂ > IE₁ always

Successive ionisation energies always increase, because you are pulling an electron off an increasingly positive ion. What is diagnostic is where the jumps are large. A sudden spike means you have finished a shell and started breaking into the noble gas core.

The exam move

Given an unlabelled list of successive ionisation energies, find the largest ratio between consecutive values. The number of ionisations before that jump equals the number of valence electrons, which gives you the group. Sodium's IE₂/IE₁ ratio is 9.2 — unmistakable. This identifies an unknown element from energetics alone.

Two anomalies in period 2 you must be able to explain

Be (899) > B (801)

Beryllium is 1s²2s²; boron is 1s²2s²2p¹. Boron's outgoing electron comes from a 2p orbital, which is higher in energy and better shielded by the filled 2s pair than a 2s electron would be. The subshell change outweighs the extra proton.

N (1,402) > O (1,314)

Nitrogen is 2p³ — three orbitals, one electron each, maximum exchange stabilisation. Oxygen is 2p⁴, forcing the fourth electron to pair up in an already-occupied orbital. That pairing repulsion makes oxygen's fourth 2p electron easier to remove than nitrogen's third, despite oxygen's higher Z.

These two dips repeat in every period — Mg > Al and P > S in period 3 — because the cause is the subshell structure, not anything specific to those elements. Recognising the pattern is worth more than memorising the four cases.

Module 5.4

Electronegativity: who wins the shared pair

Electronegativity is the tendency of an atom in a bond to attract the shared electron pair toward itself. Unlike ionisation energy it is not directly measurable — it is a derived, relative quantity, which is why several competing scales exist.

Pauling derived his scale from bond energy anomalies. If a heteronuclear bond A–B were purely covalent, its bond energy should be the average of A–A and B–B. Real A–B bonds are consistently stronger than that average, and Pauling attributed the excess to ionic character:

Δ = E(A–B) − √[E(A–A) × E(B–B)] (geometric mean form) |χ_A − χ_B| = 0.102 √Δ with Δ in kJ/mol Anchor: χ(F) ≡ 3.98 by definition; everything else is relative to it. Worked check for HF: E(H–F) = 565, E(H–H) = 436, E(F–F) = 159 kJ/mol. Geometric mean = √(436 × 159) = 263. Δ = 565 − 263 = 302. |χ_H − χ_F| = 0.102 × √302 = 1.77. With χ(F) = 3.98 this gives χ(H) = 2.21, against the tabulated 2.20.

Why the 1.7 cutoff is a lie you should know how to correct

HF has Δχ = 1.78, above the cutoff, yet HF is unambiguously a covalent molecular gas. Meanwhile, bonding character depends heavily on the ions involved: Fajans' rules say a small, highly charged cation will polarise a large, soft anion and drag an "ionic" compound toward covalency — which is why AlCl₃ sublimes as covalent Al₂Cl₆ dimers rather than behaving like a salt. Use Δχ as a first estimate and reason from charge density when it matters.

Build analogy: tug-of-war with a shared resource

A covalent bond is two processes sharing a memory buffer. Equal electronegativity means fair scheduling. A large difference means one process monopolises access, and the other is effectively starved — that is an ionic bond. Polar covalent is the messy middle where access is unequal but the buffer is still genuinely shared.

Note the alternative scales: Mulliken defines χ = (IE + EA)/2, which is physically transparent and computable from measured quantities. Allred–Rochow computes it from the electrostatic force a valence electron experiences, χ = 0.359 Zeff/r² + 0.744. All three correlate well, which is reassuring for a quantity that cannot be measured directly.

Module 5.5

Electron affinity: the sign conventions that trip everyone

X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g) ΔH = EA₁ Convention used here: negative = energy released = favourable. Warning: many textbooks report electron affinity as a positive number meaning "energy released", which is the opposite sign convention. Always check whether a table's chlorine value reads −349 or +349. Both mean the same physical thing.

Unlike ionisation energy, electron affinity is not smooth. It has real structure worth understanding:

  • Group 2 and Group 18 are endothermic. Be, Mg, and every noble gas have positive EA — adding an electron requires energy, because it would have to enter a new, higher subshell outside a closed configuration. These elements simply do not form stable monoanions.
  • Nitrogen is anomalously poor (approximately +7 kJ/mol, effectively zero) because its half-filled 2p³ set resists the pairing that a fourth electron would force. Carbon, with 2p², accepts an electron more readily than nitrogen does.
  • Chlorine beats fluorine (−349 vs −328), as discussed in the halogen module: the second-period 2p shell is too small and the incoming electron pays a repulsion penalty. The same effect makes oxygen's EA less negative than sulfur's.
  • Second electron affinities are always endothermic. Forcing an electron onto an already-negative ion costs energy: O⁻ + e⁻ → O²⁻ requires +844 kJ/mol. The oxide ion exists in ionic solids only because lattice energy pays for it — O²⁻ is unstable as an isolated gas-phase species.

Putting it together: why NaCl forms at all

A Born–Haber cycle for sodium chloride: atomisation of Na (+108), ionisation of Na (+496), atomisation of ½Cl₂ (+121), electron affinity of Cl (−349), lattice formation (−787). Sum: −411 kJ/mol. The ionisation step is strongly unfavourable and the electron affinity does not cover it — lattice energy is what makes ionic compounds exist. Every "sodium wants to lose an electron to be like neon" explanation is skipping the term that actually does the work.

Module 6 · Relativistic effects

Why gold is yellow and mercury is liquid

In heavy atoms the inner electrons move fast enough that special relativity becomes a chemistry problem. This is not a curiosity — it is the reason for several of the most familiar material properties on Earth.

In the Bohr picture, the velocity of a 1s electron scales directly with nuclear charge:

v₁ₛ ≈ Z · α · c where α = 1/137 (fine structure constant) Hydrogen (Z=1): v ≈ 0.0073 c → relativistic correction negligible Silver (Z=47): v ≈ 0.34 c → mass 6% above rest mass Gold (Z=79): v ≈ 0.58 c → mass 23% above rest mass Mercury (Z=80): v ≈ 0.58 c → same regime Oganesson(Z=118): v ≈ 0.86 c → mass nearly doubled

Relativistic mass increase follows the Lorentz factor, and the Bohr radius is inversely proportional to electron mass:

m = γm₀ = m₀ / √(1 − v²/c²) r = (4πε₀ħ²) / (m e² Z) → r ∝ 1/m For gold: γ ≈ 1.23, so the 1s orbital contracts to about 81% of its non-relativistic radius. The contraction propagates outward: because all s orbitals must remain orthogonal to 1s, the 2s, 3s … 6s orbitals contract too. This is the direct relativistic effect.

The indirect effect, which is where the chemistry happens

Contracted s orbitals screen the nucleus more effectively. The d and f orbitals, which were already poorly penetrating, now experience less effective nuclear charge and expand, rising in energy. So in heavy elements the 6s drops and the 5d rises — the gap between them narrows dramatically.

Module 6.2

Superheavy elements and the island of stability

Everything past uranium (Z = 92) is synthetic in practice. They are made by accelerating a beam of medium-weight nuclei into a heavy target and hoping for fusion — a process with cross-sections measured in picobarns, meaning a successful event might occur once per week of continuous beam time.

Oganesson (Z = 118) synthesis: ²⁴⁸Cm + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁴Og + 2n (Dubna, 2002–2005) Total atoms ever produced: about 5. Half-life: ~0.7 ms. Calcium-48 is the workhorse projectile because it is doubly magic (20 protons, 28 neutrons) and unusually neutron-rich for a light nucleus, which helps the product land closer to stability.

Nuclear magic numbers

The nuclear shell model works like the electronic one: nucleons fill shells, and closed shells are exceptionally stable. The confirmed magic numbers are 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126. A nucleus magic in both protons and neutrons is "doubly magic" and remarkably stable — lead-208 (82 p, 126 n) is the heaviest stable nuclide in existence, and it is not a coincidence that it is the endpoint of the uranium decay series.

The island of stability hypothesis says that around Z = 114–126 and N = 184, shell closure should produce nuclei with half-lives of years rather than milliseconds. The problem is access — no available combination of beam and target produces a nucleus neutron-rich enough to reach the predicted centre. Present-day synthesis routes land on the wrong shore, and every additional neutron is exponentially harder to get.

Does the table end?

Richard Feynman's back-of-envelope argument suggested trouble near Z = 137, where a 1s electron's required velocity reaches c in the simple Bohr treatment. Proper Dirac-equation treatments push the limit further and make it depend on nuclear size rather than being a hard wall, with estimates clustering around Z = 172–173 for where the 1s level dives into the negative-energy continuum and the vacuum becomes unstable to electron–positron pair creation. Nobody expects to make anything near there. The practical limit is nuclear: half-lives are collapsing faster than synthesis techniques are improving.

Module 7.2

Supply chains, criticality, and what happens next

Periodic-table literacy now has a geopolitical dimension. The elements that matter most for electrification and computing are concentrated in very few places, not because of geology but because of processing capacity and environmental cost.

Concentration risk

  • The Democratic Republic of the Congo supplies roughly 70 percent of mined cobalt, a substantial share from artisanal operations with documented child labour.
  • Rare earth separation capacity is overwhelmingly concentrated in China, which processes the majority of global output regardless of where the ore is mined.
  • Lithium production is dominated by Australian hard-rock spodumene and the South American brine triangle of Chile, Argentina and Bolivia.
  • Gallium and indium are almost entirely by-products of aluminium and zinc refining, so their supply cannot be scaled independently of the host metal.

Where the chemistry is heading

  • Cobalt reduction: LiFePO₄ and high-nickel NMC cathodes trade energy density for supply security.
  • Sodium-ion: sodium sits directly below lithium with the same ns¹ pattern and is effectively unlimited; the penalty is a larger ion and lower cell voltage.
  • Rare-earth-free motors: switched reluctance and induction designs avoid permanent magnets entirely, at a cost in power density.
  • Urban mining: a tonne of discarded circuit boards contains far more gold than a tonne of high-grade ore. Recovery rates remain low, which is an engineering and policy failure rather than a chemical one.

The takeaway for a STEM career

Materials constraints are increasingly the binding constraint on technology, not transistor count or software. Someone who can reason from a periodic trend to a substitution candidate — "we need a transparent conductor; what else has a wide band gap and accepts degenerate doping?" — is doing work that is valuable in battery design, semiconductor process engineering, catalysis and recycling alike. That reasoning is what this lecture has been training.

Electrons occupy the lowest-energy available orbital first.

Able to react as both an acid and a base; characteristic of oxides near the metal/nonmetal staircase.

An element's ability to bond to itself in chains; carbon's defining property.

Having identical energy; the three p orbitals of a shell are degenerate in an isolated atom.

Enthalpy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron.

An atom's tendency, within a bond, to attract the shared electron pair.

Quantum-mechanical stabilisation from parallel-spin electrons in degenerate orbitals; the real driver of half-filled shell stability.

Reluctance of heavy p-block ns² electrons to bond, favouring lower oxidation states.

Reversible insertion of ions into a host lattice without destroying it; the basis of rechargeable batteries.

Energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion.

Having identical electron configurations; such species differ in size only by nuclear charge.

Energy released when gaseous ions assemble into a crystal; what makes ionic compounds thermodynamically viable.
Madelung rule
Orbitals fill in order of increasing n + ℓ, ties broken by lower n.

An element with properties between metals and nonmetals; typically a semiconductor.
Node
A region where the probability of finding the electron is exactly zero.

How close to the nucleus an orbital's electron density reaches; ordered s > p > d > f within a shell.
