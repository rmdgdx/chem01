Chemical Equations

Module 1.1 · From atoms to compounds

Before an equation can be right, the formulas must be right.

A compound is a chemical combination of elements with a constant composition and a characteristic set of properties. Its formula records which elements are present and the simplest ratio of their atoms.

Build analogy: a formula is the saved configuration of a chemical “build.” Change the subscript and you do not merely rebalance the build—you create a different substance.

The octet idea

The source module frames bonding around the tendency of many atoms to achieve a more stable outer-electron arrangement by losing, gaining, or sharing electrons. Hydrogen follows a duet rather than an octet.

bond formation → more stable valence-electron arrangement

Formula vs. coefficient

2H2 + O2 → 2H2O
Coefficient changes quantity Subscript belongs to the formula

Never change a correct subscript just to balance an equation. Changing H2O into H2O2 does not “fix” the equation; it changes water into hydrogen peroxide.

Module 1.2 · Ionic & covalent bonding

Two major bonding routes in the source module

1 · Ionic / electrovalent bonding

Electrons are transferred. Metals typically lose electrons and become cations; nonmetals gain electrons and become anions. Ionic solids form ordered crystal lattices and commonly have high melting points.

Na + F → Na+ + F− → NaF

2 · Covalent bonding

Two nonmetals share one or more electron pairs. A shared electron pair constitutes a covalent bond, and discrete molecules may result. The images were taken in the book Lecture Modules for Chemistry 1 by Baniqued et al. .

F· + ·F → F—F
Module 2.1 · What a chemical equation means

A chemical equation is compressed reaction information.

A chemical equation uses symbols and formulas rather than a full sentence to describe the changes occurring in a chemical reaction. A valid balanced equation must obey conservation of mass.

Reactants

Starting substances. Their formulas are written on the left side.

Arrow

→ means “reacts to form,” “yields,” or “produces.” It points toward the products.

Products

Substances formed by the reaction. Their formulas are written on the right side.

Magnesium+Oxygen→Magnesium oxide
2Mg(s)+O2(g)→2MgO(s)

Module 3.1 · Balancing workflow

Balancing is an inventory audit.

Your goal is the smallest whole-number coefficient set that gives identical atom counts for every element on both sides.

1

Write correct formulas first.

The source module highlights the diatomic elemental forms H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, and I₂ when these elements occur in their elemental molecular form.

2

Start with the more complex species.

Balance elements in compounds with several different elements before leaving H and O for later when practical.

3

Change coefficients only.

Use the smallest possible whole numbers in front of formulas. Never insert numbers into a formula or alter its subscripts.

4

Recount every element.

The final atom count for each element must be the same on both sides.

5

Treat unchanged polyatomic ions as units.

When a polyatomic ion appears intact on both sides, balancing the group as a unit can reduce work.

Worked example

BaCl₂(aq) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Ba(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2AgCl(s)↓

Counts: Ba 1=1, Cl 2=2, Ag 2=2, NO₃ groups 2=2.

Module 4.1 · Four reaction patterns

Reaction types are pattern recognition.

Synthesis / direct combination

A + B → AB

Two or more substances combine to form one product.

  • 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl
  • 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃
  • CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂
  • SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃

Decomposition / analysis

AB → A + B

One compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances. Heat, light, or electricity may drive the change.

  • BaCl₂·2H₂O(s) → BaCl₂(s) + 2H₂O(g)
  • 2KClO₃(s) → 2KCl(s) + 3O₂(g)↑
  • CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)↑
  • 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂(g)↑

Single replacement / displacement

A + BC → AC + B

A free element replaces another element in a compound when the free element is sufficiently active.

  • Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂
  • Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu
  • Cu + FeSO₄ → no reaction
  • Cl₂ + 2NaBr → 2NaCl + Br₂

Double replacement / metathesis

AB + CD → AD + CB

Two compounds exchange ions or groups to form two new compounds; precipitation is a common driving outcome in the source examples.

  • NaCl + AgNO₃ → NaNO₃ + AgCl(s)↓
  • BaCl₂ + Na₂SO₄ → 2NaCl + BaSO₄(s)↓
Quality-control note: the original source contains a few legacy typographic inconsistencies. Examples here are normalized to conserve atoms and use standard formula notation.
