1.1Development of Atomic Structure

Guiding question: How did we come to believe that matter is made of atoms with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by electrons?

The modern picture of the atom was built one experiment at a time. Each model fixed a problem the previous one could not explain, and each is still useful at the right level of detail — an engineer estimating the mass of a steel beam uses Dalton's atom; an engineer designing a semiconductor needs the quantum atom.

Scientist / YearKey experiment or ideaWhat it addedWhat it could not explain
Dalton (1803)Mass laws: conservation of mass, definite proportions, multiple proportionsMatter is made of indivisible atoms; atoms of one element are identical; compounds have fixed ratiosWhy atoms bond; electrical behavior of matter
Thomson (1897)Cathode-ray tube — rays bent by electric and magnetic fieldsThe electron: a negative particle far lighter than any atom; charge-to-mass ratioWhere the positive charge sits
Millikan (1909)Oil-drop experimentCharge of the electron (−1.602 × 10−19 C), hence its mass—
Rutherford (1911)Gold-foil experiment — most α particles pass through, a few bounce backSmall, dense, positive nucleus; atom is mostly empty spaceWhy electrons do not spiral into the nucleus; line spectra
Chadwick (1932)Neutral radiation from beryllium bombarded by α particlesThe neutron — explains isotopes and nuclear mass—
Bohr (1913)Hydrogen line spectrumElectrons occupy discrete energy levels; light emitted when they drop levelsSpectra of multi-electron atoms
Schrödinger, Heisenberg (1926)Wave mechanics; uncertainty principleOrbitals as probability regions; explains bonding, periodicity, and spectraCurrent model
Engineering connectionRutherford's "mostly empty space" is why X-ray and gamma-ray inspection works: radiation passes through metal welds and concrete, and only the dense nuclei scatter it. Non-destructive testing (NDT) of pipelines, pressure vessels, and bridge cables relies directly on this picture of the atom.
Check yourself — Which observation forced Thomson's model to be replaced, and why?
The back-scattering of a few α particles in Rutherford's experiment. A diffuse, evenly spread positive "pudding" could never reverse a fast, heavy α particle; only a concentrated positive nucleus can. So the positive charge (and nearly all the mass) had to be packed into a very small core.

1.2Subatomic Particles, Atomic Number, and Mass Number

Guiding question: What do the numbers around an element symbol tell you about the atom?

Two numbers define an atom:

  • Atomic number (Z) = number of protons. It identifies the element. In a neutral atom, Z also equals the number of electrons.
  • Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons. It is always a whole number and is specific to one isotope.
  • Therefore number of neutrons = A − Z.
Worked Example 1.1 Particles in an atom

How many protons, neutrons, and electrons are in one atom of iron-56, the most common isotope in structural steel?

Given
Iron, symbol Fe, A = 56. From the periodic table, Z = 26.
Required
Number of p+, n0, e−
Equation
p+ = Z; n0 = A − Z; e− = Z for a neutral atom
Solution
p+ = 26; n0 = 56 − 26 = 30; e− = 26
Answer
26 protons, 30 neutrons, 26 electrons
Check yourself — An atom has 29 protons and 36 neutrons. Write its nuclear symbol.
Z = 29 is copper (Cu). A = 29 + 36 = 65. The symbol is 6529Cu, or copper-65 — one of the two natural isotopes in the copper wiring of every building.

1.3Isotopes and Average Atomic Mass

Guiding question: If every atom of carbon has 6 protons, why is carbon's atomic mass on the periodic table 12.01 and not exactly 12?

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A). They behave almost identically in chemical reactions because chemistry is governed by electrons, but they differ in mass and, for some, in nuclear stability.

Average atomic mass

The value on the periodic table is the weighted average of the isotopic masses, weighted by natural abundance:

Average atomic mass = Σ (fractional abundance × isotopic mass)
Worked Example 1.2 Average atomic mass of chlorine

Chlorine — used for water disinfection and in PVC pipe — has two natural isotopes: 35Cl (34.969 amu, 75.76 %) and 37Cl (36.966 amu, 24.24 %). Calculate its average atomic mass.

Given
m35 = 34.969 amu, f35 = 0.7576; m37 = 36.966 amu, f37 = 0.2424
Required
Average atomic mass of Cl
Equation
M̄ = f35m35 + f37m37
Solution
M̄ = (0.7576)(34.969) + (0.2424)(36.966) = 26.49 + 8.960 = 35.45 amu
Answer
35.45 amu — matches the periodic table. Note the answer lies closer to 35 because 35Cl is more abundant.
Engineering connectionIsotopes are tools. Civil engineers date groundwater and sediments with 14C and 3H; nuclear gauges using 137Cs measure soil compaction and asphalt density on road projects; and 60Co sources radiograph welds. Chapter 3 returns to these applications.
Check yourself — Boron has two isotopes: 10B (10.013 amu) and 11B (11.009 amu). Its average mass is 10.81. Which isotope is more abundant, and roughly by how much?
11B, by a lot. Let f = fraction of 11B: 10.013(1 − f) + 11.009f = 10.81 → 0.996f = 0.797 → f ≈ 0.80. So about 80 % 11B and 20 % 10B. (Boron-10's strong neutron absorption is why boron steel is used for control rods and spent-fuel racks.)

1.4Ions: Cations and Anions

Guiding question: Why does sodium give up an electron while chlorine takes one — and how do you predict the charge?

An ion is an atom (or group of atoms) that has gained or lost electrons. Losing electrons leaves excess protons → a positive cation. Gaining electrons → a negative anion. Atoms do this to reach the stable, filled outer shell of the nearest noble gas.

Predicting the charge from the periodic table

TypeDefinitionExamples
Monatomic cationSingle atom that lost electronsNa+, Ca2+, Al3+, Fe3+
Monatomic anionSingle atom that gained electronsCl−, O2−, N3−
Polyatomic ionGroup of covalently bonded atoms with a net chargeNH4+, OH−, NO3−, SO42−, PO43−, CO32−

In an ionic compound the charges must cancel: the total positive charge equals the total negative charge. That single rule is how formulas are written — Ca2+ with Cl− needs two chlorides, so calcium chloride is CaCl2; Al3+ with O2− gives Al2O3 (2 × +3 = 3 × −2).

Engineering connectionIons carry current in solutions, which is why seawater corrodes steel faster than fresh water, why electroplating works, and why concrete reinforcement is attacked by chloride ions (Cl−) that migrate through pores. Water hardness is Ca2+ and Mg2+; softeners swap them for Na+ on an ion-exchange resin.
Check yourself — Write formulas for (a) magnesium nitride, (b) iron(III) sulfate, (c) ammonium phosphate.
(a) Mg2+ and N3− → Mg3N2 (3 × +2 = 2 × −3). (b) Fe3+ and SO42− → Fe2(SO4)3. (c) NH4+ and PO43− → (NH4)3PO4.

1.5Oxidation Numbers

Guiding question: How do we keep track of electrons in compounds where no ions actually exist?

An oxidation number (oxidation state) is the charge an atom would have if every bond were fully ionic. It is a bookkeeping device — but a powerful one. It lets you spot which atom lost electrons (oxidized) and which gained (reduced) in any reaction, which is the foundation of Chapter 2 on electrochemistry and corrosion.

Rules, applied in order

  1. An atom in its elemental form has oxidation number 0 (Fe, O2, S8, Cl2).
  2. A monatomic ion has an oxidation number equal to its charge (Na+ = +1, S2− = −2).
  3. Fluorine is always −1. Other halogens are −1 unless bonded to O or a lighter halogen.
  4. Group 1 metals are always +1; Group 2 metals always +2; Al is +3.
  5. Hydrogen is +1 with nonmetals, −1 in metal hydrides (NaH, CaH2).
  6. Oxygen is −2, except in peroxides (H2O2: −1) and with fluorine (OF2: +2).
  7. The sum of oxidation numbers = 0 for a neutral compound, and = the ion charge for a polyatomic ion.
Worked Example 1.3 Assigning oxidation numbers

Find the oxidation number of the underlined element in (a) KMnO4, (b) Cr2O72−, (c) H2SO4, (d) Fe3O4.

Given
Formulas above; rules for K (+1), H (+1), O (−2)
Required
Oxidation number of Mn, Cr, S, Fe
Equation
Σ(oxidation numbers) = overall charge
Solution
(a) (+1) + Mn + 4(−2) = 0 → Mn = +7
(b) 2Cr + 7(−2) = −2 → 2Cr = +12 → Cr = +6
(c) 2(+1) + S + 4(−2) = 0 → S = +6
(d) 3Fe + 4(−2) = 0 → Fe = +8/3 ≈ +2.67 — an average: magnetite contains one Fe2+ and two Fe3+ per formula unit.
Answer
Mn +7 · Cr +6 · S +6 · Fe +8⁄3 (mixed +2/+3)
Engineering connectionEvery corrosion problem is an oxidation-number problem. A steel pipe (Fe, 0) buried in wet soil slowly becomes Fe2O3 (Fe, +3). Cathodic protection works by attaching a metal that is even easier to oxidize (Zn or Mg "sacrificial anode"), so the electrons flow from the anode instead of from the pipe.
Check yourself — In the reaction 2Al + 3CuSO4 → Al2(SO4)3 + 3Cu, which element is oxidized and which is reduced?
Al goes from 0 to +3 (oxidized — loses electrons). Cu goes from +2 in CuSO4 to 0 (reduced — gains electrons). S and O stay at +6 and −2; the sulfate ion is a spectator.

1.7Chemical Equations and Balancing

Guiding question: How does a chemical equation express the law of conservation of mass?

A chemical equation is a sentence written in formulas: reactants → products. Because atoms are neither created nor destroyed (Dalton, Lavoisier), the number of atoms of each element must be identical on both sides. We enforce this with coefficients — never by changing subscripts, which would change the substance itself.

SymbolMeaningSymbolMeaning
→"yields"; reaction direction(s)solid
⇌reversible; equilibrium(l)liquid

A reliable balancing routine

  1. Write correct formulas for all reactants and products (get the chemistry right first).
  2. Balance elements that appear in only one reactant and one product first — usually metals, then nonmetals other than H and O.
  3. Balance hydrogen, then oxygen last (they often appear in several compounds).
  4. If a fraction appears, multiply every coefficient by the denominator to clear it.
  5. Check every element again and reduce coefficients to the smallest whole-number ratio.
Worked Example 1.4 Combustion of propane (LPG)

Propane, the main component of LPG used in Philippine kitchens and industrial burners, burns in oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water. Write the balanced equation.

Required

Equation

Solution
Carbon: 3 C on left → 3 CO2.
Hydrogen: 8 H on left → 4 H2O.

1.8Energy in Chemical Change

Guiding question: Where does the heat of a burning fuel come from, and how do we measure it?

Energy is the capacity to do work or transfer heat. Chemical energy is stored in bonds: breaking bonds costs energy, forming bonds releases it. If the new bonds in the products are stronger than the old bonds in the reactants, the difference is released as heat — the reaction is exothermic. If the reverse is true, energy must be supplied — endothermic.

Units

  • Joule (J) — SI unit; 1 J = 1 kg·m²/s² = 1 N·m.
  • Calorie (cal) — 1 cal = 4.184 J (heat to raise 1 g water by 1 °C).
  • kWh — 1 kWh = 3.6 × 106 J (the unit on your Meralco bill).
  • BTU — 1 BTU ≈ 1055 J (air-conditioner and boiler ratings).

Forms of energy engineers convert

  • Chemical → thermal (combustion), electrical (batteries, fuel cells)
  • Thermal → mechanical (turbines, engines)
  • Electrical → chemical (electrolysis, charging)
  • Nuclear → thermal (Chapter 3)

The heat change at constant pressure is the enthalpy change, ΔH, reported per mole of reaction as written. It scales with amount: burning twice as much fuel releases twice the heat. That proportionality is the bridge to stoichiometry in §1.12.

Worked Example 1.6 Heat from a fuel

Natural gas is mostly methane. Complete combustion releases 890 kJ per mole of CH4. How much heat does 1.00 kg of methane release? Express the answer in MJ and kWh.

Given
m = 1.00 kg = 1000 g CH4; ΔH = −890 kJ/mol; M(CH4) = 16.04 g/mol; 1 kWh = 3600 kJ
Required
Heat released, q
Equation
n = m / M; q = n × |ΔH|
Solution
n = 1000 g ÷ 16.04 g/mol = 62.3 mol
q = 62.3 mol × 890 kJ/mol = 5.55 × 104 kJ = 55.5 MJ
55.5 × 103 kJ ÷ 3600 kJ/kWh = 15.4 kWh
Answer
≈ 55.5 MJ ≈ 15.4 kWh per kg of methane (its "calorific value" — Chapter 4 develops this fully)
Engineering connectionCement kilns run the endothermic calcination CaCO3 → CaO + CO2, which is why cement manufacture is energy-intensive and a major CO2 source (both from fuel and from the reaction itself). Concrete curing, by contrast, is exothermic — large dam pours need cooling pipes to stop thermal cracking.
Check yourself — Is dissolving ammonium nitrate in water (used in instant cold packs) exothermic or endothermic? What sign does ΔH have?
Endothermic — the pack gets cold because the process absorbs heat from its surroundings. ΔH is positive (about +25 kJ/mol).

Derived units you will use constantly

  • Volume: 1 L = 1 dm³ = 1000 mL = 1000 cm³; 1 m³ = 1000 L.
  • Density: ρ = m/V — g/cm³ (= g/mL) in the lab, kg/m³ in design. 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³. Water ≈ 1.00 g/cm³; steel ≈ 7.85; aluminum ≈ 2.70; concrete ≈ 2.4.
  • Temperature: K = °C + 273.15; °F = 1.8(°C) + 32. Only kelvin is used in gas-law and thermodynamic equations.
  • Pressure: 1 atm = 101.325 kPa = 760 mmHg = 14.7 psi.
  • Energy: 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ; 1 cal = 4.184 J.

Significant figures

A measurement's digits carry information about its precision. The rules:

RuleExampleSig. figs

Dimensional analysis (the factor-label method)

Multiply the given quantity by conversion factors — fractions equal to 1 — arranged so that unwanted units cancel diagonally. If the units come out right, the arithmetic is usually right too.

A cylindrical steel shaft has diameter 40.0 mm and length 1.20 m. Steel density is 7.85 g/cm³. Find its mass in kg.

Given

Check yourself — A water tank holds 2.50 m³. Express this in liters and, using ρ = 1.00 g/mL, find the mass of water in tonnes (1 t = 1000 kg).
2.50 m³ × (1000 L / 1 m³) = 2500 L = 2.50 × 10³ L. Mass = 2500 L × 1000 mL/L × 1.00 g/mL = 2.50 × 106 g = 2500 kg = 2.50 t. (Rule of thumb: 1 m³ of water ≈ 1 tonne.)

1.11The Mole Concept and Molar Mass

Guiding question: Atoms are far too small to count — so how do we count them by weighing?

The mole is chemistry's counting unit, like a dozen but enormous: one mole contains 6.022 × 1023 particles (Avogadro's number, NA). It is defined so that the mass of one mole of any substance, in grams, equals its atomic or formula mass in amu. That number is the molar mass, M (g/mol).

n = m / M      N = n × NA      Vgas = n × 22.4 L/mol (at STP: 0 °C, 1 atm)

Worked Example 1.8 Mass → moles → particles

A 25.0 g sample of limestone is pure CaCO3. How many moles and how many formula units does it contain?

Given
m = 25.0 g; M(CaCO3) = 100.09 g/mol; NA = 6.022 × 1023 mol−1
Required
n and N
Equation
n = m/M; N = n NA
Solution
n = 25.0 g ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 0.250 mol
N = 0.250 mol × 6.022 × 1023 mol−1 = 1.50 × 1023 formula units
Answer
0.250 mol · 1.50 × 1023 formula units of CaCO3 (and therefore 1.50 × 1023 Ca2+ ions and 4.50 × 1023 O atoms)

Percent composition and empirical formulas

Mass percent of an element = (mass of element in 1 mol ÷ molar mass) × 100 %. For Fe2O3: %Fe = 2(55.85)/159.70 × 100 % = 69.9 % — the theoretical iron yield of hematite ore. Going the other way, dividing each element's mass percent by its atomic mass and reducing to whole-number ratios gives the empirical formula; the molar mass then scales it to the molecular formula.

Engineering connectionPlant engineers never count molecules, but they think in moles constantly: the mole ratio in a balanced equation is the only way to scale from a lab beaker to a 500-tonne reactor. Emissions reporting (kg CO2 per tonne of cement, per kWh) is stoichiometry expressed in moles.
Check yourself — How many grams of oxygen gas contain 3.0 × 1024 molecules of O2?
n = 3.0 × 1024 ÷ 6.022 × 1023 = 4.98 mol; m = 4.98 mol × 32.00 g/mol ≈ 1.6 × 10² g (160 g).

m(CaO), m(CO2)
Equation
n = m/M; n(product) = n(CaCO3) × (1/1); m = nM
Solution
n(CaCO3) = 5.00 × 105 g ÷ 100.09 g/mol = 4996 mol
m(CaO) = 4996 mol × 56.08 g/mol = 2.80 × 105 g = 280 kg
m(CO2) = 4996 mol × 44.01 g/mol = 2.20 × 105 g = 220 kg
Check: 280 + 220 = 500 kg ✓ (mass conserved)
Answer
280 kg CaO and 220 kg CO2 — 44 % of the limestone's mass leaves as carbon dioxide.
Worked Example 1.10 Reactant needed (mass → mass, non-1:1 ratio)

How many kilograms of oxygen are needed to completely burn 10.0 kg of propane? (C3H8 + 5 O2 → 3 CO2 + 4 H2O)

Given
m(C3H8) = 1.00 × 104 g; M(C3H8) = 44.10; M(O2) = 32.00 g/mol; ratio 5 mol O2 : 1 mol C3H8
Required
m(O2)
Solution
n(C3H8) = 10 000 ÷ 44.10 = 226.8 mol
n(O2) = 226.8 × 5 = 1134 mol
m(O2) = 1134 × 32.00 = 3.63 × 104 g
Answer
36.3 kg O2 — and since air is only 23 % O2 by mass, the burner must draw about 158 kg of air. This is the "air–fuel ratio" every combustion engineer designs around.

Limiting reactant and percent yield

When two reactants are mixed in non-stoichiometric amounts, the one that runs out first — the limiting reactant — fixes the maximum (theoretical) product. To find it, convert each reactant to moles of product; the smaller result wins. Real processes give less than theoretical:

% yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100 %
Quick example4.0 mol Fe and 4.0 mol O2 react by 4 Fe + 3 O2 → 2 Fe2O3. Fe would give 4.0 × (2/4) = 2.0 mol Fe2O3; O2 would give 4.0 × (2/3) = 2.67 mol. Iron is limiting; theoretical yield is 2.0 mol Fe2O3, and 1.0 mol O2 is left over.
Engineering connectionStoichiometry sizes everything: the oxygen plant for a steel mill, the lime dosage for neutralizing acid mine drainage, the chlorine feed for a water-treatment plant, and the CO2 footprint of a cement kiln. The special-topics project in Weeks 16–17 will ask you to do exactly this kind of calculation for your own engineering field.
Check yourself — Aluminum is produced by 2 Al2O3 → 4 Al + 3 O2. What mass of alumina gives 1.00 tonne of aluminum? (M: Al2O3 101.96, Al 26.98)
n(Al) = 1.00 × 106 g ÷ 26.98 = 3.706 × 104 mol; n(Al2O3) = 3.706 × 104 × (2/4) = 1.853 × 104 mol; m = 1.853 × 104 × 101.96 = 1.89 × 106 g ≈ 1.89 tonnes of Al2O3.

§Chapter Summary — One-Page Cheat Sheet

Atom

Dense positive nucleus (p+, n0) + electron cloud. Z = protons = identity; A = p + n; neutrons = A − Z.

Isotopes

Same Z, different A. Average atomic mass = Σ(fraction × mass).

Ions

Metals lose e− → cations (+group no.); nonmetals gain → anions (group − 8). Formulas: total charge = 0.

Oxidation numbers

Element 0; F −1; Group 1 +1; Group 2 +2; H +1; O −2; sum = charge. Up = oxidized, down = reduced.

Matter

Pure (element, compound) vs mixture (homogeneous, heterogeneous). Physical change keeps identity; chemical change makes new substances.

Equations

Balance with coefficients, never subscripts. Metals → other nonmetals → H → O; clear fractions.

Energy

ΔH < 0 exothermic (combustion), ΔH > 0 endothermic (calcination). 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ; 1 cal = 4.184 J.

Naming

Ionic: cation + anion (Roman numeral if variable). Molecular: prefixes + -ide. Acids: hydro-ic / -ate→-ic / -ite→-ous.

Measurement

SI units and prefixes; sig figs; dimensional analysis — arrange factors so units cancel.

Mole

n = m/M; N = n × 6.022 × 1023; 22.4 L/mol at STP. Moles are the hub of every conversion.

Stoichiometry

g A → mol A → (mole ratio) → mol B → g B. Limiting reactant gives theoretical yield; % yield = actual/theoretical.

Key equations

neutrons = A − Z  ·  M̄ = Σ fimi  ·  ρ = m/V  ·  K = °C + 273.15  ·  n = m/M  ·  N = nNA  ·  q = n|ΔH|  ·  % yield = actual/theoretical × 100
